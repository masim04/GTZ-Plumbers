Preventing Sewer Backups During Storms

10 min read • GTZ Flood Preparedness

Sewer backups during heavy rain are more than an inconvenience — they can be a severe health hazard and cause extensive property damage. Floodwaters and sewage contain pathogens, chemicals, and debris that require professional cleanup and remediation. Understanding how backups happen and taking straightforward preventive steps can dramatically reduce the risk and severity of incidents.

How Backups Develop

Backups occur when the downstream capacity to handle wastewater is exceeded or blocked. Common triggers during storms include surcharge from municipal mains, infiltration of groundwater into weakened pipes, and blockages caused by tree roots or grease build-up. Combined sewer systems — where stormwater and sewage share a pipe — are especially vulnerable during high-volume runoff events.

High-Impact Prevention Measures

  • Backwater Valves: A properly installed backwater or backflow preventer on your building sewer is among the most effective protections. It allows normal outflow but closes to stop reverse flow from the main during surcharging events.
  • Sewer Lateral Inspections: Have a camera inspection performed every few years or when you notice slow drains. Early detection of root intrusion or offsets lets you remediate before a blockage forms.
  • Hydro-jetting Maintenance: Professional hydro-jetting removes grease, scale, and roots that create blockages. Schedule periodic maintenance if your property is in a high-risk area.
  • Proper Surface Drainage: Keep gutters, downspouts, and storm drains clear and ensure they discharge away from the foundation and sewer connection. Redirecting roof runoff reduces pressure on the sewer system.
  • Sump Pumps & Backup Power: For properties prone to flooding, a reliable sump pump with battery backup reduces the risk of groundwater combining with sewer surcharges to inundate basements.

Site Planning and Landscape Considerations

Site grading, driveway and patio drainage, and impermeable surfaces all affect how water moves across a property. Ensure that hardscapes slope away from the home and that landscape features do not trap runoff near foundation drains. Consider permeable paving and rain gardens to reduce surface runoff into storm systems during intense rains.

During a Storm — What to Do

  • Limit water use in the building to reduce internal discharge to the sewer.
  • Close basement floor drains and plug unused fixtures; keep toilet lids down.
  • Monitor sump pump operation and test battery backups ahead of predicted storms.
  • If sewage appears or you suspect a backup, stop using water immediately and call a professional — do not attempt to clear a blocked municipal main yourself.

Emergency Response and Remediation

Post-backup recovery involves containment, removal of contaminated materials, thorough disinfection, and drying. Professional restoration services coordinate with technicians to ensure HVAC systems are safe, drywall and insulation are removed where contaminated, and microbial growth is prevented. Documentation and photographs are important if you plan to file an insurance claim.

Insurance & Municipal Programs

Many homeowner policies require specific riders for sewer backup coverage. Review your policy and consider adding coverage if you live in a vulnerable area. Some municipalities offer grants or cost-share programs for installing backwater prevention devices; check local resources and building department guidance.

Long-Term Resilience

Long-term resilience involves combining structural upgrades with routine maintenance: install a backwater valve, maintain your lateral, ensure proper site drainage, and schedule professional cleaning. These investments reduce the likelihood of backups and limit recovery costs if an event occurs.
